# Save the Guide — Research and Accuracy Notes

**Research status:** Complete for the current three-part story draft. This document records the facts used to shape the fiction; it is not a comprehensive survival manual.

## Research principles

1. Use the survival facts to create believable decisions and consequences, not to pause the story for a lecture.
2. Avoid unsupported exact countdowns. Cold-water and hypothermia progression varies with the person and conditions, so the script does not use the earlier placeholder “four minutes.”
3. Do not portray an expert as infallible or natural ice as predictably safe.
4. Do not portray a small fire or dry clothing as a substitute for urgent medical care when serious hypothermia is possible.
5. Keep instructions at the level needed to understand the action. The finished video should not present itself as professional medical or ice-rescue training.

## Findings applied to the story

### 1. Ice can be weak beneath an ordinary-looking snowy surface

The Minnesota Department of Natural Resources states that ice strength cannot be judged reliably from appearance alone. Ice rarely freezes uniformly; current can weaken it, especially around flowing water and outside bends, while snow can conceal conditions and slow freezing.

**Story use:** Mara checks the edge and tells Evan to wait, yet a current-weakened patch beneath snow gives way. The accident is dangerous without requiring her to behave recklessly.

Sources:
- [Minnesota DNR — Ice safety](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/safety/ice/index.html)
- [Minnesota DNR — Ice formations and conditions](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/safety/ice/ice-formations-conditions.html)

### 2. A rescuer should avoid becoming a second victim

Government ice-rescue guidance emphasises reaching or throwing from a safe position rather than going onto broken ice. A person being rescued may kick into a horizontal position while being pulled, then remain low and roll away from the opening.

**Story use:** Evan initially steps toward the hole but responds to Mara's instruction to go back. He throws the rope from the bank, braces himself, and pulls while Mara kicks horizontal. She rolls clear rather than immediately standing.

Sources:
- [U.S. Army Corps of Engineers / DVIDS — Ice Rescue Techniques](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/360091/ice-rescue-techniques)
- [Massachusetts Department of Fire Services — Ice and cold water safety](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/ice-and-cold-water-safety)
- [Minnesota DNR — Danger! Thin Ice](https://files.dnr.state.mn.us/education_safety/safety/ice/dangerthinice.pdf)

### 3. Cold water affects breathing and useful movement before a person can calmly problem-solve

The National Weather Service and U.S. Army Corps of Engineers describe cold shock and subsequent physical/cognitive impairment. The precise timeline varies, so the fiction shows gasping, loss of coordination, and clumsy fingers without claiming a universal number of minutes.

**Story use:** Mara initially struggles to breathe and cannot close her fingers around the rope. Evan's clear instruction to kick gives her a concrete action.

Sources:
- [National Weather Service — Cold Water Hazards and Safety](https://www.weather.gov/safety/coldwater)
- [U.S. Army Corps of Engineers / DVIDS — Ice Rescue Techniques](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/360091/ice-rescue-techniques)

### 4. Hypothermia impairs both movement and judgment

The CDC lists shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, and drowsiness as warning signs. Mayo Clinic notes that shivering can stop as body temperature falls; confusion and reduced shivering must not be interpreted casually as recovery.

**Story use:** Mara's broken instructions, incorrect name for Evan, and “catch the bus” statement are signs of impaired thinking. Evan initially misreads her stillness as peaceful improvement, creating the Part 1 cliffhanger.

Sources:
- [CDC — Preventing Hypothermia](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/index.html)
- [Mayo Clinic — Hypothermia: First aid](https://www.mayoclinic.org/first-aid/first-aid-hypothermia/basics/art-20056624)
- [American Academy of Family Physicians / Wilderness Medical Society — Accidental Hypothermia Guidelines](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/1101/p571)

### 5. Immediate priorities are shelter, dry insulation, gentle handling, and appropriate warming

CDC and Mayo guidance include moving to shelter, removing wet clothing, using dry layers, insulating from the ground, and warming the body's core gradually. Mayo also warns against rapid rewarming, massaging limbs, or giving alcohol. Drinks require an alert person who can swallow safely; nobody should force fluids on a confused or unconscious person.

**Story use:** Evan uses the foam pad, sleeping bag, spare dry clothing, and bivy. He keeps Mara covered while removing wet outer layers. In Part 2, he does not force a drink while she is confused and only offers one after she is alert enough to swallow.

Sources:
- [CDC — Preventing Hypothermia](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/index.html)
- [Mayo Clinic — Hypothermia: First aid](https://www.mayoclinic.org/first-aid/first-aid-hypothermia/basics/art-20056624)
- [U.S. Forest Service — Hypothermia](https://www.fs.usda.gov/visit/know-before-you-go/hypothermia)

### 6. Ground insulation and wind protection matter

Winter-camping and hypothermia guidance consistently separates a person from cold ground and wind. The emergency blanket/bivy is only one layer in that system, not a magical heat source.

**Story use:** Mara's pad is placed beneath her, dry insulation around her, and the bivy blocks wind. The fire stays outside the insulated shelter. The script does not claim that a foil-like layer alone makes her safe.

Sources:
- [Mayo Clinic — Hypothermia: First aid](https://www.mayoclinic.org/first-aid/first-aid-hypothermia/basics/art-20056624)
- [Princeton Outdoor Action — Winter Camping Guide](https://www.princeton.edu/~oa/winter/wintcamp.shtml)
- [Alberta Parks — Winter Survival in the Backcountry](https://www.albertaparks.ca/media/2161491/2008_winter_survival.pdf)

### 7. A trip plan is a backup, not an instant rescue button

The National Park Service recommends leaving route and return information with a trusted contact who can report an overdue traveller. It also warns that changing a plan without updating the contact can make search and rescue more difficult.

**Story use:** Mara has a six o'clock check-in with the lodge. Her missed call initiates the overdue-trip response, but darkness, weather, terrain, and the need to search mean rescue still takes time.

Sources:
- [National Park Service — Trip Planning Guide](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/trip-planning-guide.htm)
- [National Park Service — Trip Plan](https://www.nps.gov/articles/gtgtripplan.htm/index.htm)

### 8. “Stay or go” depends on conditions; movement is not automatically heroic

NPS wilderness guidance says to avoid leaving an injured person alone when possible and notes that bad weather can make staying put safer than travelling. If leaving becomes necessary, the injured person should be sheltered and their location clearly identified.

**Story use:** Evan wants to run for the trailhead but does not immediately do so in darkness with Mara confused and unable to travel. Part 3 uses only a short, prepared trip to a nearby meadow after conditions and Mara's state allow it.

Source:
- [National Park Service / Olympic National Park — Wilderness Safety](http://home.nps.gov/olym/planyourvisit/wilderness-safety.htm)

### 9. Rescuers need contrast and deliberate signals

NPS recommends bright clothing in a clearing and a signal mirror. Other preparedness and outdoor-safety training sources describe repeated groups of three and large, high-contrast ground signals.

**Story use:** Evan creates a dark-branch X in an open snowy meadow, places Mara's orange jacket at its centre, and uses the compass mirror and whistle when aircraft are near. The signal works because it is visible and deliberate, not because rescuers mysteriously know where to look.

Sources:
- [National Park Service / Olympic National Park — Wilderness Safety](http://home.nps.gov/olym/planyourvisit/wilderness-safety.htm)
- [Utah Be Ready — Signaling](https://beready.utah.gov/family-preparedness/12-areas-of-preparedness/communication/signaling/)
- [Hunter-Ed — Signaling for Help](https://www.hunter-ed.com/national/studyGuide/Signaling-for-Help/201099_93100/)

## Story decisions made for medical and practical credibility

- The series spans roughly one afternoon, one night, and the following morning rather than keeping a hypothermic, injured person alone for several convenient days.
- Mara remains in need of evacuation even after her condition improves.
- Evan does not diagnose a specific fracture or perform advanced treatment; he knows only that she cannot bear weight on the knee.
- The missed check-in starts a process; it does not make a helicopter appear immediately.
- The final rescue is earned through the trip plan, clothing description, clearing, contrast, and active signalling.
- A later production review should check the final narration and frames for any shot that accidentally teaches an unsafe action, even if the written script is accurate.
