# The Store Had Everything — Part 1: The Last Announcement

The store had enough food for months. Enough water. Enough batteries to light every aisle. Unfortunately, it also had a public-address system that would not shut up. Every thirty seconds, a cheerful voice announced another sale. Every thirty seconds, more infected pressed against the front glass. From behind checkout three, Nolan could see the security office above the rear wall. The announcement controls were in there. So was the switch for the metal shutter. All he and Lena had to do was cross the store before the next announcement brought the whole crowd through.

“Turn it off,” Lena whispered. “Working on it,” Nolan said. He had a store key, a box cutter, and no plan beyond reaching that door. Lena had the keys to her delivery van in the loading bay. Neither of them knew whether the van was a better bet than the building. They knew only that the glass could not take another hour of this.

They moved while the speakers held the crowd's attention. During each announcement, the infected outside turned toward the same cheerful sound. In the short gaps, some of them began noticing other things. Nolan and Lena ran during the voice and froze during the silence. Twenty-eight seconds of movement. Two seconds of stillness. It was not a good system. It was the only one they had.

Nolan led along the checkout line and into camping. Lena pulled a compact hand-crank radio from a display as they passed. The direct aisle would have been faster. It was also blocked by an infected man in a red hoodie, walking toward the front speakers. Nolan pulled Lena off the aisle and behind a stack of blue coolers. They could still see him between the orange tent poles. He was getting closer. Lena's van keys slipped from her hand.

The chime was tiny. The man turned toward the coolers. Nolan saw Lena's eyes over the rim, wide and furious at herself. Neither of them breathed. Then the next sale began. The man snapped his attention toward the front again and staggered past their hiding place. Lena swept the keys off the floor. Nolan led her around the green kayak display and toward the rear stairs. The voice that was trapping them had just saved them.

They reached the office as the announcement ended. Nolan's key missed the lock twice. In the sudden silence, every scrape of metal sounded enormous. Lena held the radio against her chest to stop its crank handle from clicking. “Nolan,” she said. He found the keyhole. The lock turned. They slipped inside and shut the door as softly as they could.

Inside, monitors glowed above the desk. Nolan's first idea was to kill every switch on the control panel. Lena caught his wrist. The shutter controls were on the same panel. “Not everything,” she said. He nodded, found the public-address amplifier, and opened its cabinet. The next sale was already counting down. He pressed stop. The countdown kept moving. So he pulled the amplifier's power lead. The cheerful voice died halfway through “everything must—”.

The silence was so complete that Nolan heard Lena's keys shaking against her palm. On the front camera, the crowd did not vanish. That would have been too easy. But no new infected were running toward the glass, and the ones at the back were beginning to turn toward other noises in the street. The immediate problem was the crowd already pushing. Nolan pressed the shutter control. The motor answered with a deep metallic groan.

“That thing is louder than the announcements,” Lena said. “It also locks,” Nolan said. They watched the steel curtain descend over the glass. Fists struck it. The whole frame shuddered. Lena reached toward the switch, perhaps to stop it, perhaps just to do something. Nolan did not know. He kept his hand beside hers without touching the controls. The shutter hit the floor. The lock engaged. Neither of them cheered.

Lena set the hand-crank radio on the desk. Her phone had shown no service since the doors locked. The radio did not need the cell network or an outlet. She turned the handle and searched for the emergency station. Static. A burst of music. Static again. Then a measured voice reading road closures and shelter instructions. Real information. Not a guess from a frightened stranger online.

The message was short. The eastern road was closed. The river road was closed. People in the district were being told to remain indoors unless an official evacuation notice directed them elsewhere. Nolan looked through the office window at rows of food, water, and camping gear. For the first time all night, staying felt like a decision instead of a surrender. Lena did not look at the shelves. She was watching the loading-bay monitor.

Her van sat inside the bay, nose toward the outer shutter. She held up the keys. “Half a tank,” she said. “Enough to get us somewhere. Not enough to wander.” Nolan hated how quickly he wanted to say no. The store was familiar. That did not make it safe. The van was movement. That did not make it a destination.

A deep bang rolled through the building. On the front camera, the steel shutter was holding, but its centre had begun to bow inward. Not much. Just enough to change the shape of the reflection. Nolan thought about the food in the aisles, the radio on the desk, and the lock at the loading bay. None of those things could tell them when the front would fail.

“We pack for the van,” Lena said. “If the shutter holds, we can unpack.” Nolan looked out at the crowded shelves. The answer was not stay or go. Not yet. It was to be ready before the building chose for them. They had stopped the voice. They had covered the glass. Now they had to decide what survival could fit into two backpacks.
