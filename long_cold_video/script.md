You wake because the apartment has gone quiet. Not empty-quiet. Wrong-quiet. The furnace in the closet by the bathroom, the one that hums all winter like a tired animal, has stopped humming. You lie still and listen to nothing for a long time.

You check your phone. Four twelve in the morning. Nineteen percent battery. The weather app is a grey circle that will not load, and you decide that means poor signal and not that the world has ended, because you are still half asleep and that is a reasonable thing to decide.

You get up to pee and the floor tiles bite the soles of your feet. That is the first thing that tells you the truth. Tile holds cold like a grudge. You have lived here three winters and never felt it before, because the apartment was always warm and you were always barefoot and nothing had ever gone wrong.

The window over the sink has frost on the inside. Frost on the inside is not weather. Frost on the inside is a message: the building has stopped fighting.

You turn the tap. It coughs, spits, and gives you a thin brown trickle, then air. The pipes are already freezing. It is four nineteen in the morning and your pipes are already freezing.

Outside, the streetlight is out. All of them are out. The buildings across the alley are black rectangles, and you realise the only light in the city is the snow, falling thick and slow under a sky the colour of an old bruise. It is not normal snow. It is falling too straight, too heavy, like it is being poured.

You put on a coat over your pyjamas and stand at the front door with your hand on the knob for a full minute before you open it.

The hallway is colder than your apartment. That is not how hallways work. Hallways are supposed to be warm.

You go back inside. You lock the door. The lock makes the small, solid click you have heard ten thousand times, and this time it sounds like the last time.

Then you do the thing you will think about for the rest of your life: you sit on the edge of the bed, in the dark, in your coat, and you wait for somebody to tell you what to do. Nobody does. Nobody will.

At six the sun comes up and does nothing. It hangs in the sky like a coin behind smoked glass, a pale thin light that casts no heat and barely any shadow. The thermometer on the kitchen window reads twenty-two below. You check it twice. You check it a third time in the light of your phone, because you have decided, without admitting it, that the thermometer must be broken.

Your hands start to be a problem around seven. You are wearing gloves that were never meant for this, gloves for scraping a windscreen, and your fingertips go from cold to numb to something worse, a dull wooden feeling, as if you are wearing somebody else's hands.

That is when you stop thinking of it as bad weather and start thinking of it as a thing that is trying to kill you.

You take inventory. This is the part they get wrong in the films. There is no montage. There is no cleverness. You stand in your kitchen with a numb hand and try to think clearly while your body insists that thinking is a luxury it can no longer afford.

Two litres of water. Three quarters of a bag of rice. A packet of peanuts. One can of soup. A dead battery bank. A first aid kit with two bandages that expired in twenty nineteen.

You take everything you can wear. Two shirts. Two jumpers. Your grandfather's wool coat, the heavy one you used to make fun of. Socks on top of socks on top of socks, because your feet will not tell you they are freezing until it is too late.

You take a lighter. A box of matches from the drawer. A paperback you have already read, and you hate yourself a little for how gently you put it in the bag.

Then you look at your phone. Nine percent, because cold eats batteries alive. You send one message to your sister. Heading south. I am going to the coast. Then you switch it off, and you will not switch it on again for eleven days.

The car does not start. Of course it does not start. It was built for a world that stopped existing at four in the morning.

You sit in the driver's seat with your hands between your thighs and you understand, very clearly, that you own three thousand pounds of useless metal.

So you walk.

The road south is eight lanes of frozen cars. They are not crashed. That is the detail that stays with you. Nobody crashed. People just stopped, because the engine died, because the traffic ahead was already dead, and they got out and started walking and left their doors open behind them.

Their doors are still open. That is how little time there was.

You keep your head down and you keep moving, because stopping is how you die. It is that simple, and it stays that simple for the next nine days. Stopping is dying. That is the whole new religion.

You try the door of a house with a Christmas wreath still up. Inside it is colder than it is out on the street, which makes no sense and perfect sense at the same time. You take a tin of peaches, a wool blanket, and the photograph in the frame by the stairs. Then you put the photograph back, because you cannot carry a stranger's family and your own cold at the same time.

You find a gas station at dusk. The power is gone, so the pumps are dead, but there is a stack of firewood outside the door, and behind the counter there is a man who has been dead for a few hours, sitting up, in a jacket, as if he was resting his eyes.

You do not scream. You do not touch him. You take a lighter from the rack, and afterwards you are ashamed of what you take and what you leave.

You build a fire in the room behind the counter, in a metal bucket, with the firewood he would have sold you for six dollars. You feed it one page at a time. And when it is going, you sit as close as you can get without burning your boots, and you cry properly for the first time, in short ugly hot spurts, and then you stop, because crying costs water, and because somewhere in the last hour you made a decision to survive him.

The second day is the longest day of your life. The third is worse.

Here is the maths nobody teaches you. You burn through your body's fuel like a stove. You need three times the food you have been eating, and you have food for maybe five days. Warmth is measured in hours of paper. Water freezes in the bottle by mid-morning, so you learn to carry it inside your coat, against your stomach, where it is uncomfortable and where it melts.

You learn to walk in twenty minute pieces, because after twenty minutes your fingertips go white and your toes stop reporting in. You learn to clap your hands like an idiot in the middle of a frozen street, and by the fourth day you have stopped caring how you look, because looking good is a thing for people with a roof.

You learn that shivering is a good sign. Actually that is the worst thing you learn all week. When the shivering stops, you are in trouble. When you feel warm, you are in real trouble. When you start thinking how nice it would be to sit down, just for a minute, right here in the snow, that is your body shutting down and offering you a polite exit.

You remember that. It saves your life twice.

You sleep in a church on the sixth night. There is a stove. There are candles. There is a noticeboard with a bake sale announcement still pinned to it, which is somehow the worst thing that has happened to you so far.

You do not pray. You were not raised to. But you sit in the front pew with your boots off and say out loud, to nobody, the names of everyone you love, and the sound of your own voice in that cold building frightens you more than the dark ever did.

And in the morning, lacing your boots with fingers that belong to somebody else, you hear an engine.

It is a pickup truck. It is real. It is coming down the road with a plough on the front and one headlight out, and you step into the middle of the road and wave both arms until your shoulders burn.

The driver stops. He rolls the window down two inches.

He has a beard full of frost, a chipped tooth, and a tank that is three quarters full. He also has a rifle in the rack and a look in his eyes that says he has been through the same eleven days as you and come out with less left over.

He asks if you have family further south. You say a sister. He nods like that is a good answer, and does not ask anything else, and you understand that in his arithmetic a person with somewhere to go is a person worth a gallon of diesel.

He says he is going south. He says the coast is still warm, or at least it was, or at least that is what the last radio said. He says he will take you as far as the county line for twenty litres and something to burn.

You do not have twenty litres. You have a wedding ring in a drawer in an apartment you will never see again, a lighter with almost no fluid in it, and the one thing that actually matters: you know how to build a fire in a metal bucket out of things he has not thought of.

So you make a deal. You ride shotgun. You keep the fire going when he stops. He keeps the glove box shut. And neither of you says the word trust out loud.

The coast is warm. Not tropical. Just above freezing. Wet, grey, and full of people standing on a beach looking up at a sky that has finally started working again.

You stand in the surf with your boots in your hand and you understand that the whole world changed in the time it takes to lose a furnace.

You did not survive because you were strong. You survived because you made a thousand small decisions correctly, because you learned to treat warmth like a currency, and because on the third morning, when your body offered you that polite exit in the snow, you stood up.

The cold never got you. The cold counts on you stopping.

Do not stop.
