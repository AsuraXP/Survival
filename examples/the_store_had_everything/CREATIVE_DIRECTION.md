# The Store Had Everything — Creative Direction

Date: 2026-10-23

Status: direction, rough arc and Part 1 script approved by the user on 2026-10-23; expressive opening style selection in progress; no recurring characters or story frames locked

## Why the direction is changing

The completed _Save the Guide_ Part 1 remains preserved and on hold. Its restrained naive/dot-eye style will **not** carry into the new launch story. The user found the finished result too visually dull and the `-5%` narration less natural. The new story will use expressive characters, vivid colour and Andrew at normal **0%** speed.

## 1. New visual target: Expressive Vivid 2D

A clear, expressive 2D animated-story/graphic-novel look that remains practical to reproduce across many narrative frames.

### Required

- **Readable eyes:** visible irises/pupils and restrained highlights where the shot is close enough; eyelids, brows and gaze direction carry emotion. No default dot-eye faces.
- **Sharp expressions:** fear, doubt, urgency, relief and determination must read immediately through brows, eyes, mouth, posture and hands—not through narration alone.
- **Vivid colour with purpose:** richer blues, warm ambers, emergency reds and clean saturated accents. Avoid the previous washed-out grey-brown dominance.
- **Strong lighting and contrast:** emergency lighting, flashlight beams, lit screens and deep but readable shadows. Faces must remain legible in dark scenes.
- **Dynamic silhouettes and poses:** weight shift, reaching, recoiling, running and hesitation should be visible in the body, not only the face.
- **Clean hand-drawn/cel-style finish:** confident line work and controlled cel shading rather than the old deliberately awkward naive rendering.
- **Moment-specific compositions:** wide shots establish geography; close shots carry emotion; action shots show cause and effect.

### Avoid

- Dot eyes, blank expressions, washed-out colours, static symmetrical poses and uniformly pale backgrounds.
- Photorealism, horror gore, excessive comic-book muscularity, unreadable darkness or so much rendering detail that recurring characters cannot remain consistent.
- Captions, logos, watermarks and random readable brand text inside story frames.

### Practical consistency rule

Expressiveness must not come at the cost of identity. Once the style is selected, lock face, eye design, hair, wardrobe, colour palette and lighting rules in recurring references before producing the full story.

Spatial continuity is equally strict: same-situation frames must preserve character positions, facing, props, background landmarks, and accumulated scene damage unless the story explicitly changes them. The mandatory procedure is in `CONTINUITY_PROTOCOL.md`.

## 2. Narration and retention direction

### Opening promise

The first seconds must deliver the actual premise, not a logo or slow setup: a cheerful sales announcement, a resource-filled store, and infected gathering at the glass. The viewer should immediately understand the location, threat and unusual irony.

### Narrative engine

Each scene should contain a clear sequence:

**goal → obstacle → choice → consequence → new question**

- Start with action and a specific problem, not character biography.
- Use short sentences and varied rhythm; allow silence after a meaningful discovery.
- Reveal character through what each person notices, chooses and gets wrong.
- Pay off small questions instead of endlessly delaying every answer.
- Raise the next problem through the consequence of the previous decision.
- Avoid generic hype such as “you won't believe what happens next.” The situation itself must create curiosity.
- Useful information must appear because a character needs it in that moment, never as a detached lecture.

### Voice

Use one continuous `en-US-AndrewNeural` take at **0%**. Review the actual opening audio before full production. Normal speed is the current user-approved choice; do not silently change it.

## 3. Grounded-fiction rule

The infected outbreak and its behaviour are fictional rules to establish consistently. The emergency decisions around them should remain plausible.

We may invent characters, dialogue, store geography, timing and the infected threat. We should **not** invent random claims about real equipment, communications, first aid, evacuation or sheltering and present them as reliable information.

For this premise, candidate real-world actions to research and embed naturally include:

- Prioritising a small, mobile kit instead of carrying every available item.
- Water, nonperishable food, flashlight, batteries, first aid and a battery/hand-crank radio as genuinely useful emergency categories.
- Knowing exits and avoiding a route that becomes more dangerous during an evacuation.
- Following trustworthy emergency information rather than rumours.
- Choosing shelter or evacuation according to the actual threat instead of treating “always stay inside” or “always run” as universal rules.
- Conserving power and avoiding unnecessary attention while maintaining communication options.

Fictional infected behaviour must never be labelled as real disease guidance.

## 4. Research sources

### Hook and retention

- [YouTube Creator Playbook: the first 15 seconds](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-creator-playbook-tips-first-15/) — capture attention early, make the subject clear and tease where the video is going.
- [Four tips to hook your viewers on YouTube](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/four-tips-to-hook-your-viewers-on/) — questions, immediate context and cold openings can create an effective hook.
- [Understand your content performance for YouTube's recommendation system](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559650?hl=en) — concise hooks should deliver the title/thumbnail promise, followed by storytelling that builds anticipation and curiosity.
- [Measure key moments for audience retention](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop) — after publication, inspect intro retention, top moments and dips rather than guessing which sections work.

### Practical emergency information

- [Ready.gov — Build a Kit](https://www.ready.gov/kit) — core emergency categories include water, food, radio, flashlight, first aid, batteries and signalling supplies; work and vehicle kits also matter.
- [Ready.gov — Evacuation](https://www.ready.gov/evacuation) — follow official instructions, leave early enough to avoid being trapped and take an emergency kit.
- [Ready.gov — Shelter](https://www.ready.gov/shelter) — shelter decisions and protective actions depend on the emergency and available official information.
- [CDC — Build an Emergency Kit](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/psa-toolkit/build-an-emergency-kit.html) — first aid, fire extinguisher, battery radio, water, nonperishable food, flashlight and batteries are useful preparedness categories.
- [CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-create-and-store-an-emergency-water-supply.html) — water-storage guidance for real emergencies; verify any quantities before using them in narration.

## 5. Proof-before-production plan

1. Finish the rough multipart arc and complete Part 1 script with the hook and scene-level choices written for Andrew at 0%.
2. Test the new expressive visual direction with a small number of style candidates; do not generate the full story yet.
3. Lock the selected style and recurring character/location references.
4. Produce a **30–45 second opening pilot** with the actual narration, word-locked frames and hard cuts.
5. Review the pilot for expression clarity, colour, voice, hook and pacing before producing the remaining frames.
6. Continue only after the pilot proves that the combination is engaging.

This checkpoint is intended to prevent another large frame investment before the finished audiovisual result has been judged.
