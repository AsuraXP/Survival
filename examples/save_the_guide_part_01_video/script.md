# Save the Guide — Part 1: The River

The survival instructor was chest-deep in freezing river water, and her student had forgotten how to move. Mara Voss was clawing at a shelf of broken ice. Evan Reed stood on the bank, staring at the rope in his hands. Then Mara shouted, “Rope!” Not help. Not his name. Just the next thing he needed to do. What happened after that would decide whether either of them left Blackpine Valley alive.

Six hours earlier, Evan had asked Mara how she stayed so calm. They were taking a break beneath the spruces while she made him build a fire in the wind. His fingers shook. His first arrangement collapsed, and he snapped at her. Mara didn't comfort him. She pointed at the scattered twigs. “Panic spends energy,” she said. “Spend it on the next thing.” Then she made him gather every branch and begin again.

Evan was twenty-eight, an office worker who triple-checked doors and avoided arguments. He had booked the two-day winter course because he was tired of freezing whenever life stopped following the plan. Mara was thirty-six, with fourteen winters of guiding behind her. She never pretended nothing could go wrong. She showed him where the spare clothes were, how the throw rope worked, and why she checked in with the lodge at six every evening.

By their final afternoon, Evan had managed a small fire, a crooked shelter, and three shaky compass bearings. He was starting to feel proud. Then the trail reached a narrow, slow side channel of the Blackpine River. Snow covered the ice. Mara stopped, tested the edge, and told Evan to wait on the bank. She would cross first. He would follow her exact line. The ice looked no different where she stepped. It wasn't.

Under the snow, a ribbon of current had thinned the ice near the far bank. Mara's right leg went through first. Then her whole body dropped. Her pack dragged sideways beneath the broken shelf before she could free the straps. Inside it were the group shelter, the stove, her sleeping bag, and the satellite messenger. Evan understood all of that later. At the river, he saw only her hands.

The cold knocked the breath out of her. She tried to pull herself up, but the edge broke again. Evan took one step toward her and felt the ice crack under his boot. “Back,” Mara gasped. “Rope. From the bank.” His mind went blank. Then he heard her lesson inside the panic. Next thing. He dropped to his stomach on solid ground, untangled the throw rope, and tossed the loop toward her. The first throw fell short.

His second landed beside her elbow. Mara looped it around her arm, but her fingers wouldn't close. “Kick,” Evan shouted. “I've got you.” It was the first useful thing he had said all weekend. He braced his boots against a spruce root and pulled while Mara kicked herself horizontal. For a second, she slid backward. Then her chest reached the ice. Evan kept pulling. When she was clear of the hole, she rolled toward him instead of standing.

On the bank, Mara tried to give him instructions. Her words came out broken by shivering. Get out of the wind. Sleeping bag. Dry layers. Evan wrapped the rope around his pack, helped her into the spruce trees, and lowered her onto his foam pad. Her right knee had struck the ice edge. When he asked if she could walk, she looked at it and said, “Not today.” Her wet outer clothes were already stiffening.

He opened his pack. His own sleeping bag suddenly felt too small, his spare sweater too thin, his emergency bivy too flimsy. Every object looked useless because he was asking it to save her by itself. Mara made him start again. Wet layers off. Dry insulation around her. Pad beneath her. Bivy blocking the wind. Evan worked quickly, keeping her covered as he removed the wet outer layers. Then he checked his phone. No service. Five forty-two. Eighteen minutes to check-in.

He wanted to run for the trailhead, hours away through deep snow. Mara caught his sleeve before he stood. “Not in the dark,” she said. “Fire first.” He gathered dead branches from beneath the spruces, snapping them into the three sizes she had made him memorize. He built a small platform to keep the fire off the snow. His first sparks died. His second bundle smoked and collapsed. Behind him, Mara had begun calling him by someone else's name.

For a moment, Evan couldn't remember how the fire lay worked. The same blankness as the river. He almost stood up and ran. Instead, he looked at his hands and asked, “What is the next thing?” Tinder. He unpacked it and started again. A spark caught in the dry fibers. He fed it the smallest twigs, then the larger ones, until a real flame lifted through the wood.

When he turned back, Mara was watching the fire. “You did it,” she whispered. Evan laughed once, surprised by the sound. He added another branch, then reached into the bivy to check her hands. She was quieter now. That seemed like a good sign. The violent shivering that had frightened him was fading. Her body looked still. Her face looked almost peaceful.

He checked his phone again. Five fifty-one. Somewhere beyond the valley, evening was coming on. Back at the lodge, six o'clock was coming too. If Mara missed that call, her contact would raise the alarm. They only had to last until someone found them. Evan repeated that thought because it sounded manageable. A few hours. A small fire. One injured person to keep warm.

He moved closer to Mara. “What comes next?” he asked. She opened her eyes. For several seconds, she looked straight through him. Then she said, “We should catch the bus.” There was no bus for fifty miles. There was no road. Evan felt the smile disappear from his face. Mara wasn't shivering anymore. And getting her out of the river had only been the beginning.
