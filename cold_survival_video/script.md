You wake up cold.

Not winter cold. Wrong cold. The kind of cold that slips under the door and waits for you in the dark.

Your breath hangs in the air of your own bedroom. You sit up. The blanket feels thin as paper.

The radiator under the window is dead cold. You touch it twice, like that will change it. It doesn't.

Your phone is already screaming. Forty-seven notifications. Then ninety. Then the number stops mattering, because every screen says the same word.

Evacuate.

Below it, a timestamp from four hours ago. You slept straight through the end of the old world.

You turn on the news, and the news turns on you.

A volcano nobody had ever heard of has torn itself open across the ocean. Not one vent. A chain of them. A zipper of fire, unzipping the crust of the Earth.

The anchor's voice is too calm. That is how you know.

Behind her, a map keeps redrawing itself. Red dots bloom along a fault line like a rash.

Ash fell on three continents overnight. The dust is still climbing.

On the screen, scientists keep repeating the same two words like a prayer they do not believe: volcanic winter.

Volcanic winter. Not a season. A sentence.

They say the dust will circle the globe in nine days. They say the temperature will keep falling for years.

They say stay indoors, seal your windows, wait for instructions.

Then the broadcast stutters, skips, and the anchor is gone, replaced by a gray test card and a tone that goes on and on.

You call your mother. It rings once and fails. You call again. It rings once and fails.

The network is not broken. The network is full. Everyone on Earth is saying goodbye at the same time.

You type a message instead, short and useless and true: I'm okay. I love you. I'm coming eventually.

It sits there with one check mark, and you put the phone face down, because you cannot watch it anymore.

You walk to the window. The sun is wrong.

It hangs there pale and shrunken, a lightbulb behind a dirty sheet.

Snow is falling. It is July.

You catch a flake on your palm. It is not snow. It is gray. It is ash, and it is cold, and it does not melt.

Down on the street, cars are leaving in packs.

Somebody's alarm is going off three houses down, and nobody is coming to turn it off.

A man stands in the middle of the road, just looking up, phone forgotten in his hand.

A woman loads boxes into a van, throwing in things no one needs: a lamp, a guitar, a photo album, a lamp again.

A kid's bicycle lies in the gutter, wheel still turning, slow and pointless, like a clock with nowhere to be.

Nobody is yelling. That is the strangest part. The end of the world is quiet, and orderly, and terrible.

The thermometer on your windowsill reads four degrees. Yesterday it read thirty-one. The arrow of the world is pointing down.

You do the math you never wanted to do. If it fell twenty-seven degrees in one night, what will a week cost?

You look around your bedroom. Your stupid posters. Your half-packed gym bag. Your life.

And you understand, with a quiet that is almost gentle, that none of it was built for this.

Then something in you shifts. Not hope, exactly. Refusal.

You are not going to be a headline. You are not going to be a statistic in the gray.

You get up. You start with the basics, the way every boring video you never watched told you to.

Water first. You fill the bathtub, the sink, every pot and mug and cracked coffee jug you own.

The tap pressure is already weak. By the last jug, it is a whisper. By the mug after that, it is nothing.

Food next. You pull everything from the cupboards onto the kitchen floor: rice, beans, cans of soup, a birthday candle, peanut butter, crackers, half a bag of coffee.

You line them up like soldiers. You count them twice. Three weeks, maybe four, if you stop eating like a person and start eating like a math problem.

Clothes. Every jacket you own, plus the one you gave to charity and took back last winter.

Blankets, sleeping bag, the stupid heavy coat your mother made you keep.

You drag it all into the smallest room in the apartment, the one with no outside wall, and you start building a fort out of mattresses and books.

You find the shoebox of batteries in the junk drawer, behind takeout menus and dead keys, and you laugh out loud, alone, in the gray light.

Flashlights, camp stove from the trip you never took, and every lighter in the house, lined up on the counter like a jury.

Tape on the windows. Towels under the doors. A nest for the long dark.

At noon, the light through the window is the color of old milk.

At one o'clock, you need something you do not have: medicine.

You dress in layers like an onion with a heartbeat, tie a scarf over your mouth, and step outside.

The street is a photograph of a street. Same buildings, same cars, but drained of color, dusted in gray, hushed.

Ash crunches under your boots like sugar.

A bus sits abandoned mid-intersection, doors open, engine off, nobody inside.

The pharmacy two blocks over has a broken window and a line of people waiting politely in front of it, which tells you everything about how fast the rules changed.

You wait your turn. You keep your hands in your pockets. You do not look anyone in the eye.

An old man ahead of you coughs into a handkerchief and shows the pharmacist a photo of his wife on a cracked phone.

You get your boxes. You pay with cash the pharmacist barely looks at.

On the way home, the first real flurries begin. Not ash this time. Snow. In July.

It lands on your lashes, on your shoulders, on the hood of a dead car, gentle as forgiveness.

The power flickers at six. Once. Twice. On the third flicker, it does not come back.

The fridge sighs its last cold breath. The router dies mid-blink. The apartment goes the particular silence of a house holding its breath.

Outside, the streetlights never come on. For the first time in your life, the city has no night shift.

You light the camp stove in the bathroom, door cracked, window cracked, exactly as the manual says.

Blue flame. Small sun. You hover your hands over it like a worshipper.

Dinner is soup from a can, eaten standing up, in the dark, and it is the best thing you have ever tasted.

You write the date on the wall in marker, above the fort: DAY 1.

Under it, after a pause, you add: STILL HERE.

The temperature inside keeps falling anyway. You watch the numbers crawl down like a tide going out.

You sleep in the fort with three jackets on and your phone at twelve percent, wrapped in a sock like a sleeping baby.

Sometime after midnight, you wake to a sound you felt more than heard.

Through the thin wall, through the dead street, through the falling gray: sirens. One, then another, then all of them, weaving into a chord that does not resolve.

You climb out and press your ear to the window glass.

The city below is a circuit board losing power, block by block, window by window, until the dark is almost total.

Almost. Because to the north, beyond the ridge, the sky is not dark.

It glows. A dull, bruised orange, pulsing slow, like a heartbeat under the skin of the world.

The mountain is still talking.

You go back to bed, but sleep is gone. So you do the only thing left that feels like control.

You wind the little hand-crank radio until your arm aches, and you turn the dial through static, through ghosts, through voices in languages you don't know.

And then, faint but real: a voice. A woman's voice, reading numbers. Coordinates, maybe. Or a list of names.

She repeats it twice, then says the sentence you will carry for the rest of your life: If you can hear this, head north to the valley. We have heat. We have walls. We have room.

Then static. Then nothing. Then, very far away, the sirens again.

You look at the fort. At the cans. At the tape on the windows.

Everything you built today was built to stay.

You look at the map on the wall, at the ridge, at the valley beyond it, two days of walking through the gray.

Everything the voice said was built for going.

You blow out the candle. In the dark, your breath hangs like a question.

Tomorrow, you decide. Tomorrow, you choose: the nest or the north.

But tonight, the ash keeps falling. Soft as snow. Silent as snow. Endless as snow.

And the temperature keeps dropping, one degree at a time, patient as debt.

This is only day one.

And the cold is just getting started.
